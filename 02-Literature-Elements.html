Module 2: Literature Elements

Reading & Literature Skills - identifying and analysing plot, setting, characterisation and other elements.

Grade 7 · English Home Language (HL) · Term 4 exam revision · 2 x 60 minutes

By the end of this module you can:

  • Define and identify the main elements of literature: plot, setting, characterisation, conflict, narrator, theme, mood.
  • Analyse how an author reveals character (direct and indirect characterisation).
  • Identify different types of conflict and explain the theme of a text.
  • Use evidence from a text to support answers.

1. Learner Notes

Plot - what happens

PartMeaning
ExpositionThe beginning; we meet the characters and setting.
Rising actionEvents that build tension and show the conflict.
ClimaxThe turning point; the most exciting moment.
Falling actionEvents after the climax; tension eases.
ResolutionThe ending where the conflict is settled.

Setting - where and when

Setting is the time and place of the story. It can shape the mood and the characters' actions (e.g. a river at dawn creates a calm or hard-working mood).

Characterisation - how we learn about characters

TypeMeaning
DirectThe narrator tells us what the character is like ('Thabo was kind').
IndirectWe judge from the character's words, actions and how others treat them.
ProtagonistThe main character we follow.
AntagonistThe person or force working against the protagonist.

Conflict - the struggle

  • Man versus man - conflict with another person.
  • Man versus self - an inner struggle (fear, guilt, a difficult choice).
  • Man versus nature - conflict with weather, animals or the environment.
  • Man versus society - conflict with rules, traditions or a community.

Narrator and point of view

Point of viewWho tells the story
First personA character in the story uses 'I' and 'we'.
Third person limitedAn outside narrator follows one character ('he', 'she').
Third person omniscientAn outside narrator knows the thoughts of all characters.

Theme and mood

  • Theme - the central message or lesson (e.g. kindness, courage, the value of education).
  • Mood - the feeling the reader gets from the text (calm, tense, joyful, sad).
  • Tone - the narrator's attitude towards the subject.

2. Interactive Practice

3. Worked Example

Passage: The New Girl

On Zanele's first day at a new school, she sat alone at the back of the classroom, gripping her pencil. A group of girls whispered and laughed. Zanele felt her cheeks burn. At break, a girl named Amara walked over and offered half of her sandwich. 'New schools are hard,' she said. Slowly, Zanele relaxed and smiled. By the end of the day, the two girls were walking home together.

Analysis

ElementExample / explanation
SettingA school, on Zanele's first day.
CharacterZanele - shy and anxious; Amara - kind and welcoming.
ConflictMan versus self (Zanele's shyness) and man versus society (feeling excluded).
ThemeKindness can turn loneliness into friendship.
MoodWorried at first, then warm and hopeful.

4. Worksheet

Passage: The Broken Bicycle

Sipho had saved for a year to buy a bicycle. He loved it more than anything. One Saturday, his cousin Mandla borrowed it and returned it with a bent wheel. At first Sipho shouted and refused to speak to him. That night, he remembered how Mandla had once shared his only blanket on a cold trip. The next morning, Sipho fetched his tools and asked Mandla to help repair the wheel. Together they straightened it before lunch.

Questions

  1. Name the protagonist and one other character. (2)
  2. Describe the setting of the story. (2)
  3. What is the conflict, and what type of conflict is it? (2)
  4. How does Sipho change from the beginning to the end? (3)
  5. Which character trait does Sipho show at the end? Give evidence. (3)
  6. State the theme of the passage in one sentence. (2)

7. Remember this!

One-glance recap - if a line feels fuzzy, scroll back to the notes.

  • Plot = what happens, in order: exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution.
  • Setting = where and when. Mood = the feeling the text gives you.
  • Characterisation = how the writer builds a character: directly, or through words and actions.
  • Theme = the big message behind the story. Every answer needs evidence from the text.

Lesson plan, differentiation, exit ticket and homework for this module.

Lesson Plan

Learning objectives

  • Define and identify the main elements of literature: plot, setting, characterisation, conflict, narrator, theme, mood.
  • Analyse how an author reveals character (direct and indirect characterisation).
  • Identify different types of conflict and explain the theme of a text.
  • Use evidence from a text to support answers.

Prior knowledge

Learners can retell a story and describe a character.

Resources

Short story / novel extract, plot-diagram handout, worksheet, dictionary.

Teaching steps

TimeActivity
10 minIntroduce the elements using a familiar story (e.g. a folk tale).
15 minModel a plot diagram and a character analysis on the board.
15 minGuided practice: learners map the plot and identify conflict in a shared story.
15 minIndependent practice: analyse theme and character in the worksheet story.
5 minExit ticket: name and explain one element.

Differentiation, Exit Ticket & Homework

Support

  • Provide a printed plot diagram and sentence starters ('The story is set in ...').

Extension

  • Rewrite the ending from Mandla's point of view, keeping the same theme.

Exit ticket

Write one sentence naming the conflict in a story you read today.

Homework

Read a short story and complete a plot diagram plus a 3-line character description.
