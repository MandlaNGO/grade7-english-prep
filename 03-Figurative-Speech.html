Module 3: Figurative Speech & Figures of Speech

Reading & Literature Skills (and Language) - understanding and applying figurative language in context.

Grade 7 · English Home Language (HL) · Term 4 exam revision · 2 x 60 minutes

By the end of this module you can:

  • Define and identify the main figures of speech: simile, metaphor, personification, alliteration, onomatopoeia, hyperbole, idiom and imagery.
  • Explain the effect of a figure of speech in context.
  • Use figurative language in own writing and speech.

1. Learner Notes

Literal vs figurative language

Literal language means exactly what it says ('The dog is brown'). Figurative language uses words for effect or to create pictures in the reader's mind ('The dog was a furry rocket').

Figures of speech

FigureDefinitionExample
SimileComparison using 'like' or 'as'The hall was buzzing like a beehive.
MetaphorComparison WITHOUT 'like' or 'as'Time is a thief.
PersonificationGiving human qualities to an object or ideaThe wind whispered through the trees.
AlliterationRepetition of the same starting soundPeter picked purple petals.
OnomatopoeiaA word that imitates a soundCrash! The plates fell.
HyperboleDeliberate exaggerationI've told you a million times.
IdiomA phrase with a figurative meaningShe let the cat out of the bag.
ImageryWords that appeal to the sensesThe warm bread smelled of honey.
SymbolAn object that stands for a bigger ideaA dove = peace.

Explaining the effect

Do not just name the figure - explain what picture it creates and why the writer used it. Example: 'The hall was buzzing like a beehive' creates the picture of a busy, noisy place and shows the excitement of the market.

3. Worked Example

Lines from a poem

The moon is a silver coin above the town.

The tired road yawns and stretches into the night.

Snap, crackle, pop went the fire.

Analysis

LineFigureEffect
The moon is a silver coinMetaphorMakes the moon seem bright, round and precious.
The tired road yawns and stretchesPersonificationMakes the road seem alive and the journey long and weary.
Snap, crackle, popOnomatopoeiaLets the reader hear the fire crackling.

7. Remember this!

One-glance recap - if a line feels fuzzy, scroll back to the notes.

  • Simile compares using like/as. Metaphor says one thing IS another.
  • Personification gives human actions to non-human things.
  • Alliteration = same beginning sounds. Onomatopoeia = sound words.
  • Answer recipe: name the figure of speech, explain it in your own words, then say the effect.

Lesson plan, differentiation, exit ticket and homework for this module.

Lesson Plan

Learning objectives

  • Define and identify the main figures of speech: simile, metaphor, personification, alliteration, onomatopoeia, hyperbole, idiom and imagery.
  • Explain the effect of a figure of speech in context.
  • Use figurative language in own writing and speech.

Prior knowledge

Learners can distinguish literal from non-literal language.

Resources

Poem extract, figures-of-speech chart, worksheet, dictionary.

Teaching steps

TimeActivity
10 minIntroduce literal vs figurative language with everyday examples.
20 minTeach the named figures with the table; learners find examples in a poem.
15 minGuided practice: identify and explain the effect of figures in context.
10 minCreative practice: learners write 3 of their own figurative sentences.
5 minExit ticket: identify one figure of speech.

Differentiation, Exit Ticket & Homework

Support

  • Give a matching card game (figure + example) for pair work.

Extension

  • Write a four-line poem containing at least three different figures of speech.

Exit ticket

Identify the figure of speech: 'The leaves danced in the breeze.'

Homework

Find five figures of speech in a song or poem and label each one.
