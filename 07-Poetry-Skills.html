Module 7: Poetry Skills

Reading & Literature - how to read, annotate and answer questions on poems.

Grade 7 · English Home Language (HL) · Term 4 exam revision · 2 x 60 minutes

By the end of this module you can:

  • Explain how a poem is built: lines, stanzas and typography.
  • Identify rhyme, rhythm and sound devices in a poem.
  • Tell the difference between mood and tone.
  • Find the theme and message of a poem.
  • Annotate an unseen poem using the SIFT method and answer questions with quotes.

1. Learner Notes

How a poem is built

  • Line - one row of words in the poem.
  • Stanza - a group of lines, like a paragraph in prose.
  • Typography - the way words are placed on the page (spacing, shape, capital letters).
  • Punctuation - poets choose it on purpose: a full stop is a long pause, a dash is a rush.

Sound in poetry

  • Rhyme - words ending with the same sound (day / away).
  • Rhyme scheme - the pattern of rhymes, e.g. abab (line 1 rhymes with 3, line 2 with 4).
  • Rhythm - the beat of the poem. Read it aloud to feel the beat.
  • Alliteration - repeated beginning sounds (slippery, slimy slugs).
  • Assonance - repeated vowel sounds (slow golden glow).
  • Onomatopoeia - words that imitate sounds (buzz, crash, sizzle).
  • Repetition - a word or line repeated for emphasis.

Mood and tone

TermWhat it isExamples
MoodThe feeling the poem creates in the READERcalm, excited, sad, hopeful, tense
ToneThe poet's ATTITUDE towards the subjectadmiring, playful, angry, serious, nostalgic

Theme and message

  • Theme - the big idea: nature, friendship, courage, change, loss, hope.
  • Message - what the poet wants us to think about or do.

The SIFT method (annotation in four steps)

LetterStepAsk yourself
SSubjectWhat is the poem about, in one sentence?
IImageryWhich pictures do I see, hear, smell or feel?
FFigurative languageSimiles, metaphors, personification, sound devices?
TTone and ThemeHow does the poet feel? What is the big idea?

How to annotate a poem

  1. Read it silently; then listen while it is read aloud.
  2. Number the lines so you can quote quickly (e.g. 'line 3').
  3. Circle figures of speech; underline striking words.
  4. Write the mood in the margin; draw arrows from key words to your notes.
  5. Write a one-sentence summary at the bottom (your SIFT 'S').

2. Interactive Practice

3. Worked Example

Model poem: The Baobab

1 Grey giant of the dusty plain,

2 you hold the sky on knotted arms.

3 Your bark is like wrinkled leather

4 that remembers a hundred storms.

5 When the sun is a hammer on the earth,

6 your shade is a cool green pool.

7 Children race to your feet

8 and climb the ladder of your roots.

Model annotation

LineFeatureWhat it adds
1Personification - 'Grey giant'The tree seems strong and alive.
2Personification - 'hold the sky'The tree is powerful, like a support for the world.
3Simile - 'like wrinkled leather'Old, tough, weathered bark.
4Personification - 'remembers'The tree is a keeper of history.
5Metaphor - 'the sun is a hammer'The heat is heavy and punishing.
6Metaphor - 'your shade is a cool green pool'The shade is refreshing, like water in a desert.
7-8Imagery and actionThe tree is part of community life.

Model answers

Q: What figure of speech is 'the sun is a hammer on the earth'? (1)

A: A metaphor - the sun is compared to a hammer without using 'like' or 'as'. (SIFT step F.)

Q: What mood does the poem create? Quote to support. (2)

A: A calm, admiring mood - 'your shade is a cool green pool' makes the tree feel safe and refreshing. (2)

Q: What is the theme? (1)

A: Nature's strength and endurance - the tree survives 'a hundred storms'. (1)

4. Worksheet

Poem: Rain at Night

1 The rain taps the roof like small fingers,

2 whispers down the gutters,

3 drums on the classroom tin,

4 and the whole world smells of green.

5 I lie in the dark and listen

6 to the river learning to roar.

Questions (10 marks)

  1. What is the poem mainly about? (1)
  2. Name the figure of speech in 'The rain taps the roof like small fingers'. (1)
  3. Quote TWO words that create the sound the rain makes. (2)
  4. What mood does the poem create? Support your answer with a quotation. (2)
  5. What does 'the river learning to roar' suggest about the rain? (2)
  6. Do you think the last line is about more than the river? Explain your answer. (2)

Work on your own, then check the answer key below.

6. Exam-style Question & Memo

Paper 2, Q1 style - Poetry (10 marks, about 15 minutes)

Read the poem and answer the questions that follow.

Poem: Morning Market

1 The market wakes before the sun.

2 Mangoes blush in wooden crates,

3 a vendor's voice rolls like a drum,

4 and smoke from grills curls through the gate.

5 Bright coins sing into a tin,

6 as the city strolls slowly in.

  1. Identify the figure of speech in line 2: 'Mangoes blush in wooden crates'. (1)
  2. Explain the simile in line 3: 'a vendor's voice rolls like a drum'. (2)
  3. Quote a word from the poem that shows the market is full of sound. (1)
  4. What mood does the poem create? Support your answer with a quotation. (2)
  5. What does 'the city strolls slowly in' suggest about how people move? (2)
  6. What does the word 'blush' suggest about the mangoes' colour? (2)
Show memo (click to reveal)
  1. Personification - mangoes cannot really blush; it suggests ripening red-orange fruit. (1)
  2. The vendor's voice is compared to a drum: deep, loud and carrying across the market. (2)
  3. Any of: 'rolls', 'sing'. (1)
  4. A cheerful, busy mood - 'the market wakes before the sun' or 'Bright coins sing into a tin'. (2)
  5. People are unhurried and relaxed - they stroll, taking in the market, instead of rushing. (2)
  6. The mangoes are ripening into warm red-orange colours, like blushing cheeks. Accept 'becoming ripe / ready to eat'. (2)

7. Remember this!

One-glance recap - if a line feels fuzzy, scroll back to the notes.

  • Stanza = group of lines. Line = one line. Count carefully if asked.
  • Sound devices: rhyme, rhythm, alliteration, assonance (same vowel sound), onomatopoeia.
  • The look of the poem matters too - spacing, shape and capitals can carry meaning.
  • SIFT the poem: Subject, Imagery, Figurative language, Tone and Theme.

Lesson plan, differentiation, exit ticket and homework for this module.

Lesson Plan

Learning objectives

  • Explain how a poem is built: lines, stanzas and typography.
  • Identify rhyme, rhythm and sound devices in a poem.
  • Tell the difference between mood and tone.
  • Find the theme and message of a poem.
  • Annotate an unseen poem using the SIFT method and answer questions with quotes.

Prior knowledge

Learners know basic figures of speech (simile, metaphor, personification) from Module 3 and can read a short poem aloud.

Resources

Learner notes, model poem, worksheet poem, exam-style poem and memo; a dictionary; highlighter.

Lesson flow (2 x 60 minutes)

  1. Session 1: How a poem is built - lines, stanzas, typography, rhyme and rhythm (notes + read-aloud).
  2. Session 1: Sound devices and mood vs tone - the class finds examples in the model poem.
  3. Session 2: SIFT annotation - teacher models 'The Baobab'; pairs annotate 'Rain at Night'.
  4. Session 2: Independent practice - worksheet questions, then the exam-style poem under time (15 minutes).

Success criteria

'I can annotate a new poem on my own and answer three or more questions with evidence from the poem.'

Differentiation, Exit Ticket & Homework

Support (scaffolding)

  • Give learners the SIFT card to hold while annotating.
  • Read the poem aloud twice before they answer questions.

Extension

  • Learners write a 4-line stanza using at least two figures of speech and label them.

Exit ticket

Write the SIFT summary of 'Rain at Night' in one sentence, then name the mood.

Homework

Choose any poem from a textbook, annotate it with SIFT and answer: subject, mood, and one figure of speech with its effect.
